# Rooms and reverb

Record someone reading a sentence in a closet, and then in a bathroom. The words are the same, but the recordings are not. The bathroom's hard walls send echoes back for a second or more, and each syllable smears into the next. The closet's clothes swallow the echoes. A recognizer trained only on closet-quiet speech will stumble in the bathroom, and it is the same speech.

To teach a recognizer that speech in a hall is still speech, you can add the hall to your training data. That needs two things: a way to make any clean recording sound like it was made in a given room, and a single number that says how reverberant that room is. The last chapter gave you the first, because a room is a filter, and filtering is convolution. This chapter builds a room, and then measures it.

## What you already know

- A linear time-invariant system is its impulse response, and filtering with it is convolution.
- FFT convolution pads both signals to at least $L_x + L_h - 1$ samples, so nothing wraps around.
- An energy ratio in decibels is $10 \log_{10}$ of the ratio, and an amplitude ratio is $20 \log_{10}$.
- Energy is amplitude squared, so an amplitude that falls by a factor of 1000 loses a factor of $10^6$ in energy, which is 60 dB.
- Gaussian noise has no pattern from one sample to the next.

## What a room does to a click

Clap once in a hall and listen. First you hear the clap itself, the sound that traveled straight from your hands to your ears. This is the **direct path**. A moment later come the echoes: sound that bounced off one wall, then two walls, then the ceiling. Each bounce takes the sound on a longer path, so it arrives later, and each bounce soaks up some energy, so it arrives quieter. After a few bounces the echoes are so many and so close together that you no longer hear separate ones. They blur into a smooth **tail** that dies away.

That sequence is the room's impulse response. Figure 1 shows a synthetic one: a spike of 1 for the direct path, then noise under an envelope that shrinks steadily. Convolve any dry recording with it, and each sample of the recording sets off its own copy of the room's answer. Add all the copies and you have the recording as heard in the room. In code that is `convolve_fft(dry, h)`, with the FFT convolution from the last chapter.

## How long the tail lasts

The number that summarizes a room is its **reverberation time**, $\text{RT}_{60}$: how long the tail takes to lose 60 dB of energy. Sixty decibels is about the gap between a shout and a quiet room. A small furnished office has an RT60 of about 0.3 s, and a concert hall about 2 s.

Sabine's formula estimates it from the room itself: $\text{RT}_{60} \approx 0.161\, V / A$, where $V$ is the volume in cubic meters and $A$ is the total absorption, each surface's area times its absorption coefficient, in square meters. A $5 \times 4 \times 3$ m room has $V = 60$ and a surface area of 94 m². With hard walls that absorb 10% of the sound, $A = 9.4$ and $\text{RT}_{60} \approx 1.03$ s. With soft furnishings at 30%, $A = 28.2$ and it falls to 0.34 s. Absorbing more energy at each bounce makes the tail die faster, which is why the closet is dry and the bathroom is not.

## Build a synthetic room

A real response needs measurement or a simulation of every wall. For training data you can use a simpler model that has the two features you need: a direct path, and a tail that loses 60 dB in a chosen time. Take white noise for the tail, because a dense mass of echoes has no pattern, and shape it with an exponential envelope.

Work out the envelope first. Losing 60 dB of energy is a factor of $10^6$, so the amplitude falls by a factor of $10^3$. An exponential $e^{-c t}$ reaches one thousandth at $t = \text{RT}_{60}$ when $c \cdot \text{RT}_{60} = \ln 1000 = 6.908$. So:

$$
h[n] = 0.1\, \nu[n] \, e^{-6.908\, n / (f_s \, \text{RT}_{60})}, \qquad h[0] = 1
$$

Read it aloud: each sample is Gaussian noise $\nu[n]$ scaled by 0.1 and by an envelope that reaches one thousandth after $\text{RT}_{60}$ seconds, and the first sample is replaced by a direct-path impulse of 1. The 0.1 sets how loud the tail is next to the direct path. On average the direct path is 5.4 dB quieter than the whole tail at an RT60 of 0.3 s, and 11.4 dB quieter at 1.2 s. That is why a large room sounds distant: more of its energy comes from the reflections.

```python
t = np.arange(n) / sample_rate
h = 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(n) * np.exp(-np.log(1000.0) * t / rt60)
h[0] = 1.0
```

## Measure the RT60

Suppose someone hands you an impulse response and asks for its RT60. The obvious approach is to square the response, convert to decibels, and read off when it falls 60 dB. This fails for two reasons. A squared noise sequence jumps up and down from sample to sample, so the curve is far too ragged to read a time from. And the end of a real response is buried in the recording's own noise, so it never reaches 60 dB below the start.

The fix is **Schroeder integration**. At each moment, add up all the energy that is still to come, and plot that in decibels. A sum over many samples averages out the jumps, so the curve is smooth, and it starts at 0 dB and only falls, because there is less and less energy left. The formula is the following:

$$
E(n) = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\sum_{m \ge n} h[m]^2}{\sum_{m \ge 0} h[m]^2}
$$

Read it aloud: at sample $n$, sum the squares of the response from $n$ to the end, divide by the total, and convert to decibels. In numpy, a reversed running sum does the integration:

```python
energy = np.cumsum((h ** 2)[::-1])[::-1]
decay = 10 * np.log10(energy / energy[0])
```

Work it on $h = [1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125]$. The squares are $1, 0.25, 0.0625, 0.0156$, which add up to $1.3281$. The energy still to come at each sample is $1.3281$, $0.3281$, $0.0781$, and $0.0156$. Dividing by the total and taking $10 \log_{10}$ gives $0$, $-6.07$, $-12.30$, and $-19.29$ dB (Figure 2).

Even the smooth curve doesn't reach $-60$ dB reliably in a real recording, so nobody measures the whole fall. Fit a straight line to the curve between $-5$ dB and $-25$ dB, which skips the direct path at the start and the noise at the end, and extrapolate the line to $-60$ dB. > [!KEY]
> Reverb is convolution with a room's impulse response. RT60, the time for its energy to fall 60 dB, is the slope of the Schroeder decay curve, measured over 20 dB and extrapolated.

Convolving clean speech with measured or synthetic room responses is a standard training augmentation for recognizers. Room responses can also be simulated with the image-source method, which mirrors the source in every wall.

> [!RECAP]
> - A room is an impulse response: a direct path, then a dense tail of reflections that decays roughly exponentially.
> - RT60 is the time for the tail to lose 60 dB of energy, and Sabine's formula estimates it as $0.161\,V/A$.
> - A synthetic tail is `0.1 * noise * np.exp(-np.log(1000.0) * t / rt60)` with the first sample set to 1.
> - Schroeder integration is `np.cumsum((h ** 2)[::-1])[::-1]` in decibels, and it is smooth because it sums the energy still to come.
> - RT60 is minus 60 over the slope of a line fitted between $-5$ and $-25$ dB.

## Build it step by step

The function `convolve_fft` is provided, because you built it in the last chapter. The checks test yours in this order.

1. **`schroeder_decay_db(h)`** reverses, cumulates, reverses back, divides by the first value, and takes `10 * np.log10`. A common mistake is `20 * np.log10`, but this is energy, which is already squared.
2. **`estimate_rt60(h, sample_rate, start_db, stop_db)`** builds the decay curve, keeps the samples between the two levels, and fits `np.polyfit(t, decay, 1)`. A common mistake is fitting against sample index instead of seconds, which gives an answer in samples.
3. **`synthetic_rir(rt60, sample_rate, seconds, seed)`** builds the envelope with `np.exp(-np.log(1000.0) * t / rt60)`, draws noise from `np.random.default_rng(seed).standard_normal`, and sets sample 0 to 1. A common mistake is `np.log(60)`, which confuses decibels with an amplitude ratio.

## Where this breaks

**Synthetic rooms are too simple.** Real rooms have distinct early reflections, a decay that depends on frequency, and noise. A recognizer trained only on exponential noise tails can still fail in a real hall, so mix in measured responses where you can.

**The measurement range matters.** A real response ends in noise, and a fit that reaches into it sees a flat floor and reports a longer time. Choose the range above the noise floor, which is why the default stops at $-25$ dB.

**Levels change.** Convolving with a room adds the tail's energy, and the output can clip. Normalize after convolving, as the **Run** block does, and compare to a target that is aligned in time: a real direct path is delayed by the source distance.
