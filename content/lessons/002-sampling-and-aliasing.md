# Sampling and aliasing

A dataset arrives as 48 kHz audio, and your speech model wants 16 kHz. The shortest fix is `x[::3]`: keep every third sample and drop the rest. You run it, and a recording that had a clean hiss at 10 kHz now has a clear whistle at 6 kHz. Nothing in the original sound was at 6 kHz. Where did the whistle come from?

The whistle is an **alias**, and it appears whenever you sample a tone that wiggles faster than your sample rate can follow. Samples are snapshots, and between two snapshots a fast tone can do things the snapshots never see. This chapter shows exactly what the snapshots report, gives you a one-line formula that predicts the false frequency, and builds a resampler that removes the fast tones first so that no whistle appears.

## What you already know

- A tone is $A \cos(2\pi f t + \varphi)$, and its sample $n$ is taken at time $n / f_s$.
- You can build that array with `sample_tone` from [What sound is](/lessons/001-what-sound-is). This chapter gives it to you in full.
- A cosine does not care about the sign of its angle: $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$.
- A weighted moving average replaces each value with a weighted sum of its neighbors, and `np.convolve(x, h)` computes one with weights `h`.

## Two tones that give the same samples

Sample a cosine of amplitude 0.3 at 7 kHz, with a sample rate of 10 kHz. Between two samples the angle advances by $2\pi \times 7000 / 10000 = 1.4\pi$ radians. The first six samples are 0.3, $-0.0927$, $-0.2427$, 0.2427, 0.0927, and $-0.3$.

Now sample a 3 kHz cosine of the same amplitude at the same rate. Each sample advances the angle by $0.6\pi$. An advance of $1.4\pi$ is the same as $1.4\pi - 2\pi = -0.6\pi$, because a full turn of $2\pi$ puts you back where you started. And a cosine does not care about the sign of its angle. So the 3 kHz samples are the same six numbers. Figure 1 shows both curves through the same dots.

That is the whole problem. A player has only the samples, and it assumes the slowest tone that fits them, so it plays 3 kHz. The tones at $f$ and at $f - k f_s$, for any whole number $k$, are indistinguishable once you sample at $f_s$.

## Predict the alias

The false frequency is the distance from the tone to the nearest whole multiple of the sample rate. Divide the frequency by $f_s$, round to the nearest whole number $k$, subtract $k f_s$ from $f$, and drop the sign:

$$
f_a = \left| f - f_s \cdot \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right) \right|
$$

Read it aloud: take the tone's frequency, subtract the multiple of the sample rate that is closest, and keep the size of what is left. The result always lies between 0 and $f_s / 2$, the **Nyquist frequency**. In numpy it is one line, `abs(f - fs * np.round(f / fs))`.

The following table works the formula for a few tones.

| Tone | Sample rate | $k$ | Alias |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 kHz | 10 kHz | 1 | 3 kHz |
| 9 kHz | 16 kHz | 1 | 7 kHz |
| 10 kHz | 16 kHz | 1 | 6 kHz |
| 25 kHz | 16 kHz | 2 | 7 kHz |
| 440 Hz | 16 kHz | 0 | 440 Hz |

The running example, 440 Hz at 16 kHz, has $k = 0$ and keeps its frequency. Figure 2 plots the whole rule. Below 8 kHz, the shown frequency equals the tone's. Above 8 kHz, it folds back down like a zigzag, and the 10 kHz tone from the opening lands at 6 kHz.

Why not sample fast enough, so that nothing aliases? You do, if you can. The sampling theorem says that when a signal has no energy at or above $f_s / 2$, its samples determine it exactly. Speech carries most of its meaning below 8 kHz, so 16 kHz is enough for the speech. The trouble is energy you did not plan for: a hiss at 10 kHz, or a dataset recorded at a higher rate than your model expects. Any energy above $f_s / 2$ lands at a false lower frequency and stays there.

> [!KEY]
> Samples taken at $f_s$ cannot tell a tone at $f$ from one at $f - k f_s$. Anything above $f_s / 2$ must be removed before you sample, or it comes back as a false lower tone.

## Remove the fast tones first

The remedy follows from the problem. Before you drop samples, delete everything above the new Nyquist frequency with a **low-pass filter**, a filter that passes low frequencies and blocks high ones. Real converters do this in hardware before sampling, as an **anti-aliasing filter**. When you resample audio that is already digital, you do it in software.

Changing the rate by a ratio $L/M$ takes three steps, and each has a reason:

1. **Upsample by $L$.** Insert $L - 1$ zeros after every sample. The signal is $L$ times longer, which gives the filter room to place new samples between the old ones.
2. **Low-pass filter.** Remove everything above the lower of the two Nyquist frequencies, which also removes the unwanted copies of the spectrum, called **images**, that the zeros created. Multiply the filter by $L$, because the zeros diluted the signal.
3. **Downsample by $M$.** Keep every $M$-th sample. This is safe, because the filter already removed anything that could alias.

Going from 48 kHz to 16 kHz is $L/M = 1/3$, so there is nothing to upsample and the filter's job is to protect the every-third-sample step. Going from 16 kHz to 44.1 kHz is $441/160$.

## Build the filter: a windowed sinc

What does the ideal low-pass filter look like? Its weights are a **sinc**, $\operatorname{sinc}(u) = \sin(\pi u) / (\pi u)$, which is 1 at $u = 0$ and wiggles forever in both directions. An infinite filter cannot run, so you cut it to $K$ **taps**, the filter's coefficients. But cutting a sinc off sharply causes ripples in the response, because the abrupt ends are themselves a jump. So you taper the ends smoothly to zero with a window $w[n]$. This chapter uses the **Kaiser window**, `np.kaiser(K, beta)`, whose shape parameter $\beta$ trades sharpness for leakage:

$$
h[n] = 2 f_c \operatorname{sinc}\!\big(2 f_c \, (n - \tfrac{K-1}{2})\big) \, w[n],
\qquad n = 0, \dots, K-1
$$

Read it aloud: tap $n$ is the ideal low-pass response with cutoff $f_c$, in cycles per sample, centered on the middle tap, times the window. After windowing, divide by the sum of the taps so that a constant signal passes through unchanged.

```python
m = np.arange(num_taps) - (num_taps - 1) / 2
h = 2 * cutoff * np.sinc(2 * cutoff * m) * np.kaiser(num_taps, beta)
h = h / h.sum()
```

### Work a five-tap filter by hand

Take $K = 5$ taps and $f_c = 0.25$ cycles per sample, which keeps frequencies up to a quarter of the sample rate. The offsets from the middle are $m = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2$, and the ideal taps $0.5 \operatorname{sinc}(0.5 m)$ are 0, 0.3183, 0.5, 0.3183, and 0. The Kaiser window with $\beta = 5$ is 0.0367, 0.5529, 1, 0.5529, and 0.0367. Their products are 0, 0.1760, 0.5, 0.1760, and 0, which sum to 0.8520. Dividing by that sum gives 0, 0.2066, 0.5869, 0.2066, and 0.

Notice the shape: a peak in the middle that falls off symmetrically. Each output of the filter is a weighted average of the nearest inputs, which smooths away fast changes and keeps slow ones. That is what a low-pass filter is.

A symmetric filter of $K$ taps delays the signal by $(K-1)/2$ samples. SciPy's `resample_poly` uses $K = 2 \times 10 \max(L, M) + 1$ taps and $\beta = 5$. For 48 kHz to 16 kHz, $K = 61$ and the delay is 30 samples, so the resampler skips those 30 samples before it starts keeping every third one, and the output lines up with the input.

Figure 3 measures that 61-tap filter. It passes everything below 7 kHz within 0.25 dB, gives $-6$ dB at the 8 kHz cutoff, and has crushed a 10 kHz tone by 57 dB, so the whistle from the opening is gone.

> [!RECAP]
> - A tone at $f$ shows up at $|f - f_s \operatorname{round}(f / f_s)|$, which is at most $f_s/2$.
> - Anything above the new Nyquist frequency must be removed before you drop samples.
> - A windowed-sinc low-pass filter is the sinc centered on the middle tap, times a Kaiser window, divided by its sum.
> - Resampling by $L/M$ is zero-stuffing by $L$, low-pass filtering with gain $L$, skipping the filter delay, and keeping every $M$-th sample.

## Build it step by step

The checks test the functions in this order. `sample_tone` is provided.

1. **`alias_frequency(frequency, sample_rate)`** returns a float in $[0, f_s/2]$. Use `np.round` and `abs`. Common mistake: `f % fs`, which returns 9 kHz for a 25 kHz tone at 16 kHz instead of 7 kHz.
2. **`lowpass_taps(num_taps, cutoff, beta)`** returns the windowed sinc from `np.sinc` and `np.kaiser`, divided by its sum. Common mistake: `np.sinc(np.pi * u)`, because `np.sinc` already includes the $\pi$.
3. **`resample(x, up, down, half_width)`** returns `ceil(len(x) * up / down)` samples. Reduce the ratio with `math.gcd`. Build a filter with `half_width * max(up, down)` taps on each side of the middle and a gain of `up`, zero-stuff, and convolve with `np.convolve`. Then keep every `down`-th sample, starting after the filter delay. Common mistake: starting at sample 0, which shifts the output by the filter delay and fails the comparison with `resample_poly`.

## Where this breaks

**The filter has a transition band.** A 61-tap filter cannot drop from full gain to silence at exactly 8 kHz. An 8.5 kHz tone survives at $-14.3$ dB and aliases to 7.5 kHz. More taps narrow the band, at a higher cost.

**Libraries resample without telling you.** `librosa.load` resamples to 22,050 Hz unless you pass `sr=None`. A 16 kHz model then fails quietly, so print the rate of every clip you load.

**Resamplers disagree slightly.** SciPy, torchaudio, and SoX use different windows and lengths, and their outputs differ at about the $10^{-3}$ level. Compare resampled audio with a tolerance or a signal-to-noise ratio, never sample by sample across libraries.

**The ends are only half filtered.** The first and last $(K-1)/2$ input samples see only half the filter, so the checks skip 200 output samples at each end.

## The exercise

You build `alias_frequency`, `lowpass_taps`, and `resample`. Press **Run** to hear a 7 kHz tone sampled at 10 kHz come out at 3 kHz. Then hear a sweep to 16 kHz brought down to a 16 kHz rate two ways: by every third sample, where it turns around at 8 kHz, and by your resampler, where it fades out.

The checks test `alias_frequency` against a table, against the samples of the sampled tone, and against the spectrum of the sampled tone. They test `lowpass_taps` against the five-tap example and `scipy.signal.firwin`, and `resample` against `scipy.signal.resample_poly`. The last two checks need a resampled 1 kHz tone at least 50 dB SNR against a synthesized one, and a 10 kHz tone at least 40 dB below what naive decimation leaves.
